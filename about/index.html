01

THE BEGINNING

Building things.

It started with a pile of LEGO.

Around 2017, I discovered robotics through LEGO Mindstorms EV3. I started building robots for line-following and sumo competitions, learning mostly by trial, error, and breaking things that had worked perfectly a few minutes earlier.

Eventually I moved to VEX and then to Arduino. I started designing small robots and rovers of my own, learning CAD along the way and discovering how electronics, mechanics, and software had to fit together to make something actually work.

I also experimented with controlling those systems from my phone and, little by little, with making them behave without being directly controlled.

I did not have the vocabulary for it yet, but I was already becoming interested in autonomy.

ROBOTICS ARDUINO CAD EARLY AUTONOMY
02

ANOTHER WAY TO BUILD

Turning ideas into software.

Then I discovered that I could build without hardware.

Programming became another way of turning ideas into something real. With a computer, some code, and enough curiosity, I could create things without needing a workshop or a circuit board.

I started learning Java and Android Studio and built AuxiApp, my first application, focused on providing first-aid guidance. The project received an honorable mention at Infomatrix LATAM 2017.

After that, I became interested in game development. During the pandemic, I started learning Unity and C#, using game development as a way to explore programming, creativity, and my fascination with space.

One of those experiments became Space Industries. It was not really about making a game as much as it was about discovering how far an idea could go when I had the tools to build it myself.

SOFTWARE ANDROID UNITY C#
03

ENGINEERING WITH A PURPOSE

Making technology useful.

But building things was not enough.

For my school graduation project, I returned to application development with a different question: could something I built actually make someone else's life easier?

I developed an educational application to support mathematics, physics, and chemistry classes, some of the subjects that students around me found most difficult.

The project received recognition as the Best Graduation Project.

More than the award itself, the project changed the way I thought about technology. Building something was satisfying, but solving a real problem made the process much more meaningful.

04

THE FIRST ROCKET

From code to flight.

Eventually, I wanted to build something that had to work.

When I entered university and began studying aerospace engineering, many of the things I had been exploring separately suddenly had a common language.

Physics explained the motion. Electronics provided the interface with the physical world. Programming gave the system behavior. And engineering provided a way to bring all of those pieces together.

From my first semesters, I was already experimenting with water rockets and Arduino-based parachute deployment systems.

I eventually joined Volta, where I became Mission Director and lead avionics engineer for CER-0, the first major rocket I helped design and build.

I was responsible not only for the flight computer and avionics, but also for coordinating aerostructures, recovery, testing, schedules, and the overall mission.

The flight computer had to determine when the rocket had reached apogee and autonomously trigger the parachute deployment sequence. There was no room for a system that only worked in simulation.

CER-0 reached approximately 620 m against a 500 m target, completed a successful flight, and was recovered within a 50 m radius in fully reusable condition.

It was the first time I experienced the difference between writing something that works and engineering something that has to work.

05

FROM SYSTEMS TO TEAMS

Building systems became building teams.

One project led to a bigger question.

After CER-0, I became increasingly interested not only in how individual subsystems worked, but in how complete systems came together.

As Mission Director for MarQuub, a PocketQube-class satellite project, I coordinated a multidisciplinary team working across structures, power, telecommunications, avionics, embedded systems, and a soil-detection payload.

With a very small form factor and limited resources, every subsystem affected the others. A decision in one part of the architecture could create problems somewhere else.

This was my first real exposure to thinking at the system level.

Later, as Technology Manager at Volta, the scale grew again.

I helped restructure the technology division into Guidance, Navigation & Control, Telecommunications, and Embedded Systems & Flight Software, while continuing to work directly with avionics, flight computers, recovery systems, and telemetry.

I also trained 12 new members across programming, electronics, simulation, flight software, and engineering fundamentals.

I was no longer only learning how to build systems. I was learning how to build the people and structures behind them.
SYSTEMS ENGINEERING LEADERSHIP GNC EMBEDDED SYSTEMS
06

THE QUESTION CHANGED

The question was no longer "can I build it?"

It became: "Can it figure out what to do?"

Working on flight computers, CubeSat systems, telemetry, and embedded software gradually pushed me toward GNC and autonomous systems.

I started working more deeply with state estimation, sensor fusion, control, guidance, and simulation.

A system could have sensors, actuators, processors, and carefully designed algorithms, but none of those pieces were useful in isolation.

The interesting problem was the loop between them: sensing the environment, estimating what is happening, deciding what should happen next, and acting on that decision.

That became the direction I wanted to explore further: autonomous systems capable of operating when the information available to them is incomplete, uncertain, or changing.
STATE ESTIMATION SENSOR FUSION CONTROL AUTONOMY
07

LOOKING BEYOND EARTH

Taking the question somewhere harder.

More recently, my work has moved toward cislunar space, astrodynamics, and autonomous rendezvous, proximity operations, and docking.

Operating around the Moon introduces a different class of problems. The dynamics become more complex, navigation and communication constraints become more important, and autonomous decisions can have consequences that are much harder to recover from.

My current work brings together many of the things I have gradually explored throughout this journey: aerospace engineering, astrodynamics, GNC, simulation, software, and systems engineering.

I am particularly interested in autonomous systems that have to make decisions without having complete information, perfect models, or unlimited communication with the outside world.
